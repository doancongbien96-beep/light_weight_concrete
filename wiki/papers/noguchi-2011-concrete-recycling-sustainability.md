# Minimizing Environmental Impact and Maximizing Performance in Concrete Recycling

## Summary

A review comprehensively organizing the development history of concrete recycling technology in Japan from the standpoint of recycled aggregate quality and environmental impact (CO₂ · waste) (University of Tokyo Noguchi group). It covers the path from simple crushing to scrubbing-based production of high-quality recycled aggregate, the concept of completely recyclable concrete (CRC) that circulates all constituent materials in a closed loop after demolition, and technology that simultaneously resolves the trade-off between strength and recovery rate via aggregate surface modification + microwave heating.

## Key Contributions

- Organization of the genealogy of Japanese recycled aggregate standards: the 1977 construction-industry-association draft (fine-aggregate absorption limit of 13 %) → the 1994 provisional criteria (Ministry of Construction) → the three-grade system of JIS A 5021 (class H)/A 5022 (class M)/A 5023 (class L). Class H, with density of 2.5 g/cm³ or more and absorption of 3.0 % or less, is on par with natural aggregate.
- Classification of recycling technologies: mechanical scrubbing (eccentric-type mills · screw mills), heating scrubbing (embrittling the attached paste with hot air at about 300 °C). Impurity inclusion limits (tile · brick 2.0 %, gypsum 0.1 %, etc., totaling 3.0 %).
- Proposal of the CRC concept: (1) cement-recovery type — designing all constituent materials to serve as cement raw materials so they are re-cementified after calcination (recycled cement density 3.13 g/cm³, specific surface area 3340 cm²/g, with recycled concrete at W/C 0.58 reaching f'c 35.2 MPa); (2) aggregate-recovery type — recovering original aggregate free of attached paste by surface modification (a chemical method using heavy-oil coating, a physical method using a water-soluble resin film).
- Advanced aggregate-recovery type CRC: coating the aggregate surface with epoxy + silica fume/by-product powder to instead raise bond strength (strength +20 % or more), then heating only the interface locally with microwaves (2.45 GHz, 1800 W, 90 s) for recovery. An aggregate recovery rate of about 93 % was obtained in the SP80 mix.

## Methods

- Statistical and standards literature survey (Japan's environment white paper, the government waste survey) + recycled aggregate production-process data + CRC material experiments (two aggregate types, gravel · crushed stone × W/C 40·60 %, chemical/physical surface modification, two-stage crushing followed by ball-mill scrubbing).
- Microwave experiment: ∅50×100 mm specimens after 28 days of curing were heated at 1800 W for 90 s → jaw crusher → rubbing in a Los Angeles abrasion tester. CO₂ emissions were estimated per tonne processed.

## Results

- Japan's water balance: construction accounts for half of the annual total water input of 2 billion t, with concrete alone at 500 million t (about 50 % of construction resource consumption). Demolished-concrete waste is about 35 million t per year, approximately 8 % of all waste in Japan.
- CO₂: about 0.75 t per tonne of Portland cement (CaCO₃ decarbonation 60 %+fuel 30 %), and about 0.25~0.45 t per m³ of concrete. Concrete-related industries worldwide account for about 7~10 % of anthropogenic CO₂.
- Recycling rate: the recycling rate of concrete lumps already exceeded 95 % in 2000. However, since virtually all of it goes to road base-course use (downcycling), this was not qualitative circulation.
- CRC simulation: replacing 5 % of concrete with CRC every year makes demolished concrete plunge after peaking 50 years later, while CO₂ emissions decline continuously from 30 years onward.
- Advanced CRC: with silica fume at 10~20 % (of the powder), compressive strength is +20 % or more versus untreated (interpreted as a +30 % contribution from the pozzolanic reaction), while the elastic modulus drops -10 % because of the epoxy layer. Under microwave heating the aggregate recovery rate is about 93 % (overwhelmingly better than the unheated O1 case).
