# Resident Perception and Reaction to Floor Impact Noise in Apartments (Qualitative Study)

## Summary

A building-acoustics/psychoacoustics paper (University of Liverpool Acoustics Research Unit + Korea Institute of Civil Engineering and Building Technology) that conducted semi-structured in-depth interviews with 14 residents of apartments in Seoul (5 men, 9 women) and analyzed them with the grounded theory methodology to propose a conceptual model explaining the perception of and reaction to floor impact noise. **Judged unrelated to the wiki topic (ECC/cementitious composite materials)** — the paper deals neither with the physical properties of noise sources nor with floor structure performance, focusing instead on non-acoustic factors (attitudes, noise sensitivity, past experience). The "Lee" in the filename is not the first author but second author Pyoung Jik Lee (corresponding author, Liverpool), a different person from Bang Yeon Lee (Chonnam National University).

## Key Contributions

- A conceptual model structuring floor impact noise perception and reaction into 4 categories (noise exposure / noise perception / noise reaction / intervening conditions). It hypothesizes a reciprocal relationship between perception (annoyance, disturbance) and reaction (coping, health complaints), extending existing environmental noise models (Guski, Lercher, Stallen) to indoor neighbor noise.
- Most noise sources are heavy-weight impact sounds such as footsteps (10/14 participants), and sleep disturbance from night/morning noise is the most frequent complaint (8/14) — an explanatory cause is Korea's indoor shoe-free culture leading to the dominance of heavy-weight impact noise.
- Temporal sequence of coping strategies established: initially avoidant and cognitive coping; as the noise persists, escalation through indirect complaints (security office, management office) to direct confrontation and official complaints (police, Environmental Dispute Mediation Committee).
- Discovery of a new theme: retaliatory noise from the upstairs unit when relations deteriorate, while empathy toward upstairs residents lowers annoyance levels and complaint frequency.

## Methods

- A heterogeneous group of 14 participants recruited via an online announcement (ages 21~55, Seoul and vicinity, 5 owner-occupied · 9 rental), 30~90 min semi-structured interviews with consent, recording, and anonymity assured.
- Manual coding following Corbin-Strauss grounded theory procedures + recoding in NVivo 10; theoretical saturation confirmed by lack of new insights after the 13th participant, followed by one additional interview.

## Results

- Final code frequencies: annoyance 128, attitudes (neighbors) 131, boundary-setting coping (complaints) 114, avoidant coping 93, disturbance 117, heavy-weight impact 86 vs light-weight impact 61, retaliatory noise 33.
- Noise sensitivity can be raised by repeated exposure, situational changes (e.g. newborns), and sensitive family members — an extension of the traditional view of it as a stable personality trait.
- Groups with successful complaint experience showed low annoyance and positive emotions, whereas groups without outcomes showed high annoyance and strong negative emotions — coping success/failure feeds back into noise annoyance. Health complaints (stress, weight loss, dizziness, headaches, etc.) appeared frequently in self-reported form.
