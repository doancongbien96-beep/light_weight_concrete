# Water Permeability of ECC — The 100 μm Crack-Width Threshold and Self-Healing

## Summary

ECC M45, designed by micromechanics so that the peak crack opening width of the bridging stress curve stays below 100 μm, was tensioned in uniaxial tension to 1.5~3 % strain and its water permeability coefficient was measured. Because the cracks saturate at a width of about 60 μm, the permeability coefficient remained on the order of 10⁻¹⁰ m/s nearly independent of the tensile strain level (1.5 %, 2 %, 3 %) — up to 6 orders of magnitude lower than reinforced mortar cracked up to the code-permitted crack widths (300~350 μm). When normalized by the number of cracks, the per-crack permeability converged to the same order as the uncracked state. The study also showed that self-healing via calcium carbonate crystal formation proceeded during testing under a flowing water head, causing the permeation rate to decrease asymptotically. A seminal paper of ECC durability research demonstrating that ECC's tight microcracking can turn post-cracking durability into a material property decoupled from structural crack control (rebar ratio).

## Key Contributions

1. **Quantitative consolidation of the crack width–permeability correlation**: reconfirmed Wang et al.'s existing data (concrete crack widths 100 → 500 μm raise the permeability coefficient from 1.0 × 10⁻¹¹ to 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m/s, roughly a 7-order-of-magnitude increase) and adopted as a design target the threshold that below 100 μm the permeability remains similar to uncracked concrete even when cracked.
2. **Integration of the permeability constraint into micromechanics design**: beyond the energy criterion (J′b ≥ Jtip = Km²/Em) and strength criterion (σ0 > σfc, satisfied at every potential crack plane for saturated multiple cracking), presented a design procedure that itself caps the peak opening width δ0 of the bridging stress-opening curve σ(δ) at 100 μm or less. Explicitly states that crack width is a material property determined not by the reinforcement ratio but by the matrix-fiber interaction, arguing for the decoupling of durability design from structural design.
3. **Experimental demonstration of low permeability independent of strain level**: after straining to 1.5 %, 2 %, and 3 %, the permeability coefficients were 1.95 × 10⁻¹⁰, 3.00 × 10⁻¹⁰, and 7.74 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s, respectively. Although the number of cracks grew from 47 to 87, the width saturated at 50~63 μm so the increase was small, and normalization by crack count yields 4.15~8.90 × 10⁻¹² m/s per crack — essentially identical.
4. **Proposal of the normalized permeability per crack concept**: a conversion method that makes existing single-crack specimen data comparable with multi-crack ECC data. Under normalization, one 60 μm ECC crack agrees reasonably with Wang et al.'s single 60 μm concrete crack (1.0 × 10⁻¹¹ m/s).
5. **Observation of self-healing under flow conditions**: during the permeability tests, white crystals formed on and around the cracks (ESEM analysis showed mainly calcium carbonate), and the permeation rate decreased asymptotically. Healing requires flow water containing carbonate/bicarbonate and a CO₂ supply; it does not occur under mere 14-day immersion saturation or complete submergence (only localized healing near the water surface). Unhydrated binder from high binder content and low water-binder ratio is the material source of self-healing.
6. **Critique of design codes and service-life prediction models**: at the ACI 300 μm and AASHTO 350 μm crack-width limits, permeability already increases by 5~6 orders of magnitude (≈1.0 × 10⁻⁵ m/s) relative to sound concrete, so crack-control rebar alone is insufficient for durability. Points out that the uncracked assumption of service-life models such as Life-365 conflicts with reality (early-age cracking is unavoidable), and that wide cracks create macrocell corrosion potentials promoting pitting corrosion and cover spalling.

## Methods

- **Mix proportions** (kg/m³): ECC M45 = cement 578 + silica sand 462 (Class F fly ash 694, water 319, HRWR 7.51, PVA fiber 26 = 2 volume%). No coarse aggregate; silica sand mean particle size 100 μm, maximum 200 μm. Control mortar = cement 614 + Ottawa sand 1534 + water 215 with steel mesh embedded (reinforcement ratio varied 1.9~2.8 % to vary crack width and spacing). Hobart pan mixer.
- **PVA fibers**: nominal tensile strength 1620 MPa (apparent 1092 MPa), diameter 39 μm, length 12 mm, elastic modulus 42.8 GPa, elongation 6.0 %; surface treated with 1.2 % by weight of hydrophobic oil to tune interfacial properties for strain hardening.
- **Micromechanics parameter verification**: single-fiber pullout tests (displacement-controlled, after 14-day moist curing) obtained P(δ) curves → σ(δ) computed. Mortar-only prisms (75 × 38 × 300 mm, central notch 2 mm wide × 30 mm deep, 14-day air curing) tested in flexure for the crack-propagation load → Km computed. For M45, J′b/Jtip ≈ 1.08 (interpreted as a lower bound since the model omits matrix spalling under two-way pullout and inclined fiber pullout).
- **Tensile and permeability specimens**: plate cross-section 12 × 75 mm, length 300 mm (gauge length 180 mm). 14-day air curing → target strains imposed by uniaxial tension (1.5 %, 2 %, 3 %) → 14 days underwater saturation → permeability test at 28 days. Testing was performed with load removed (crack widths about 10 % closed, 5~10 μm, versus loaded state); crack widths measured at 30 locations by optical microscope.
- **Permeability test apparatus**: falling-head (for low permeability, k = (a·L/A·tf)·ln(h0/hf)) and constant-head (k = V·L/(A·h0·tf)) methods used together. Specimen sides epoxy-sealed to enforce one-dimensional flow. Geometric constants a = 2.84 × 10⁻⁵ m², L = 0.012 m, A = 8.93 × 10⁻³ m².

## Results

| Series | Crack width (μm) | Number of cracks | Permeability coefficient (m/s) | Normalized per crack (m/s·crack) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R/M uncracked | 0 | 0 | 4.58 × 10⁻¹¹ | 4.58 × 10⁻¹¹ |
| R/M-a | 150 ± 15 | 17 | 3.02 × 10⁻⁸ | 1.77 × 10⁻⁹ |
| R/M-b | 240 ± 18 | 11 | 7.97 × 10⁻⁶ | 7.24 × 10⁻⁷ |
| R/M-b | 300 ± 10 | 9 | 2.58 × 10⁻⁵ | 2.87 × 10⁻⁶ |
| R/M-c | 500 ± 50 | 5 | 4.46 × 10⁻⁴ | 8.92 × 10⁻⁵ |
| ECC uncracked | 0 | 0 | 8.18 × 10⁻¹² | 8.18 × 10⁻¹² |
| ECC 1.5 % | 50 ± 10 | 47 | 1.95 × 10⁻¹⁰ | 4.15 × 10⁻¹² |
| ECC 2 % | 52 ± 10 | 59 | 3.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ | 5.08 × 10⁻¹² |
| ECC 3 % | 63 ± 10 | 87 | 7.74 × 10⁻¹⁰ | 8.90 × 10⁻¹² |

- ECC crack spacing 2~5 mm (reinforced mortar 5~30 mm); loaded-state crack widths 40~70 μm, complying with the 100 μm design upper bound.
- The permeability of 1.5 %-strain ECC is about 5 orders of magnitude lower than equally strained reinforced mortar, and up to 6 orders lower than cracks at code-permitted widths.
- During the permeability tests the flow rate decreased asymptotically from its initial value even though the crack widths did not change — indirect evidence of self-healing. Signs of healing appeared within the first 3 days of testing, whereas no healing occurred in the saturation tank even after 14 days (interpretation: carbonate supply in the flowing water is essential).
- Conclusion: multiple fine-crack design (δ0 < 100 μm) simultaneously achieves the conflicting goals of large tensile strain and low permeability. Micromechanics-based tailored material design can be extended as a general framework linking microstructure to structural performance.
