# Carbonation Characteristics of Alkali Activated Blast-Furnace Slag Mortar

## Summary

Paper comparing the compressive strength and carbonation depth of sodium silicate powder-activated AAS mortars (G3/G5/G7, Na2O/SM 0.03~0.07) and an OPC mortar before and after accelerated carbonation (CO2 5 ±0.5 %, 7/14/21 days), and elucidating the changes in hydration products across carbonation through XRD, FTIR, TGA (DTG), and FE-SEM. As the origin paper of the user group's (Bang Yeon Lee and Jin-Kyu Song, Chonnam National University) AAS carbonation series, it explains the cause of AAS's rapid carbonation from a hydration-product perspective.

## Key Contributions

1. Simplicity of AAS hydration products: the main product is CSH alone, with almost no portlandite (Ca(OH)2) — no carbonation buffer compared with OPC. Small amounts of alumina compounds such as albite (feldspar) and a ZSM-5-type zeolite form alongside.
2. Difference in carbonation mechanism elucidated: OPC rather gains strength upon carbonation through CH consumption and pore filling by needle-shaped products (about 1.5x after 3 weeks of accelerated carbonation), whereas AAS loses strength as its CSH collapses into amorphous silica gel and the alumina compounds disappear completely, disintegrating the microstructure.
3. Buffering effect of activator dosage: increasing dosage raises CSH formation and microstructural densification, increasing strength and slowing the carbonation rate (at 7 days of accelerated carbonation G7 shows less depth than OPC, reaching a comparable level at 21 days).

## Methods

- Materials: GGBS (KS F 2563 Type 3, fineness 4,204 cm2/g, specific gravity 2.93, basicity 1.81), sodium silicate powder activator, Jumunjin standard sand (density 2.63 g/cm3).
- Mix: OPC and GGBS 94/90/86 % + Na2SiO3 6/10/14 % (G3/G5/G7), W/B 0.5, S/B 2.45.
- Curing/carbonation: 20 ±0.5 °C, RH 70 ±2 % constant temperature and humidity; from age 7 days, exposure in an accelerated carbonation chamber (CO2 5 ±0.5 %) for 7/14/21 days (KS F 2584).
- Measurement/analysis: flow and compressive strength (KS L 5105), carbonation depth (KS F 2596, phenolphthalein 1 %), XRD, FTIR (4,000~400 cm⁻¹), TGA (15 °C/min, room temperature to 1,100 °C, DTG interpretation), FE-SEM (×2,000).

## Results

- Flow: AAS higher than OPC (185~195 mm vs 170 mm), maximum for G5.
- Compressive strength (MPa, non-carbonated→carbonated, at 28 days): OPC 32.1→45.1 (increase), G3 14.9→7.4, G5 30.4→15.2, G7 42.5→28.9 (decrease). Non-carbonated G5 strength is similar to OPC.
- Carbonation depth: G3 reaches about 2.5 times OPC at 7 days and is fully carbonated before 14 days. Higher activator dosage slows carbonation; at 7 days G7 is slower than OPC.
- FTIR: the CSH Si-O asymmetric stretching band of OPC shifts from 969~979 to 1,014~1,022 cm⁻¹ (decalcification and increased polymerization). Carbonated AAS develops an amorphous silica gel band at 1,132~1,138 cm⁻¹, more amorphous than OPC (1,015 cm⁻¹) — the CSH structure of AAS itself differs from that of OPC.
- XRD: uncarbonated AAS contains abundant calcite from the outset (attributed to weathering of the raw material); albite, ZSM-5-type zeolite, and CSH confirmed. After carbonation, albite and the ZSM-5-type zeolite disappear completely and the alumina compounds vanish entirely.
- DTG: absence of the CH dehydration peak (430~480 °C) in uncarbonated AAS confirms almost no CH; mass loss concentrates in the CSH dehydration (200~400 °C) and silica gel dehydration (400~600 °C) intervals.
- SEM: in carbonated AAS the previously dense matrix collapses and exfoliates, increasing porosity. G7 shows shrinkage microcracks attributed to heat evolution from the large activator dose.
