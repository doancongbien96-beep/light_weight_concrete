# Indigenous resource utilization in lunar facility design — basalt selected as the primary material and the RESM excavation-sintering method

## Summary

An outcome of the NASA/USRA Advanced Design Program by the Sasakawa International Center for Space Architecture (SICSA) team at the University of Houston. Seven candidate construction materials for an early-phase lunar base (concrete, sulfur concrete, cast basalt, sintered basalt, glass, metals, etc.) were quantitatively compared against four criteria — resource acquisition, product properties, product usefulness, and the lunar environment — and basalt was selected as the first-priority material: a resource yield of almost 100 %, no elemental extraction required, and a simple casting process (melted at 1,320~1,350 °C followed by slow cooling). Building on this, the team presented the concept of an underground pressurized facility in which a machine performing regolith excavation and sintering simultaneously (RESM) bores a cylindrical cavity that is finished with cast-basalt panels, laid out as an 8-step construction sequence.

## Key Contributions

- Process-level grading of seven lunar construction materials: a ranking of basalt > glass > concrete > metals established on the criteria of resource-extraction energy, yield, processing time, and Earth dependence. Basalt offers a yield of almost 100 % with a curing time of about 1 day (versus 28 days for concrete).
- Quantitative basis for why a dry vacuum environment raises the tensile strength of glass: on Earth only about 1 % of the theoretical strength is realized (moisture hydrolyzes the Si-O bonds), but the Moon's dry vacuum eliminates this loss — flawless glass reaches 2,000,000 psi (about 13.79 GPa).
- Physical-property comparison table: basalt 540 MPa compression · 35 MPa tension · 110 GPa elastic modulus · 2.9 g/cm³ density · 1300 °C melting point; glass 620 MPa compression with vacuum tensile strength at the 3000 MPa class; concrete begins decomposing at 600 °C, and sulfur concrete decomposes at 115 °C — dangerously close to the maximum lunar daytime temperature.
- Shielding design figures: 2.4 m of regolith required for radiation reduction (Guerra 1988), with a finished cover of at least 3 m. Lunar surface temperatures span 111 °C ~ -171 °C. Hydrogen amounts to 50~100 g per 1 t of soil (Carter 1985).
- RESM (regolith excavation and sintering machine) concept: parallel drives for the excavation and sintering arms bore a cylindrical cavity 10~15 m in diameter and 6~12 m deep while sintering a 10~20 cm-thick basalt crust onto the walls. Microwave energy can reach 800~1,000 °C (Simonds 1988).
- Formalizing the structural advantages of underground facilities: the surrounding regolith disperses internal pressure loads, shrinks the area requiring radiation shielding, and reuses excavated soil directly as cover, minimizing energy and EVA time.

## Methods

- Evaluation criteria system: (1) resource acquisition (availability, reserves, accessibility), (2) product properties (compressive/tensile strength, elastic modulus, thermal expansion, density and radiation-shielding values, resistance to vacuum, radiation, abrasion, and micrometeorites), (3) product usefulness (breadth of applications, quality assurance, economics), (4) lunar environment (1/6 g, day-night cycle, vacuum, radiation, dust).
- Material data cited from the lunar science literature (Binder 1988, Taylor 1975, Phinney 1977, etc.) and the terrestrial cast-basalt industry (early-1900s demonstrations in France; utility pipes in Germany, the Soviet Union, and Czechoslovakia).
- Design approach: after comparing three concept families — monolithic, panel, and block — a hybrid of monolithic (sintered walls) + panel (cast basalt panels) was adopted. An 8-step construction sequence in the order of RESM excavation-sintering → underground utility core (delivered as a single package from Earth) → installation of pie-shaped cast floor and roof panels → panel fusion by pouring molten regolith into the joints → couplings and cover.

## Results

- Facility specifications: a two-story cylinder 10 m in diameter and 6 m deep (upper work deck, lower habitation deck), dual airlocks placed 180° apart on opposite diagonals, a central utility core carrying the structural support against pressure loads, and eight floor panels cast from a single mold to cut transport cost.
- Joining method: grooves cut at the abutting ends of the roof panels receive superheated molten regolith, which partially melts and fuses the surrounding cast basalt — securing monolithic behavior (Binder et al. 1990).
- Conclusion: basalt-based indigenous resource utilization satisfies the demands of both pressurized and unpressurized structures most economically, although joining technology between sintered and cast portions and the effects of the lunar environment on basalt processing remain follow-on research tasks.
