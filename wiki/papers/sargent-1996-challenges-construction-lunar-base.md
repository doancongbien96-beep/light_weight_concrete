## Summary
An immortal classic of space civil engineering, comprehensively presented at the ASCE Space 96 conference, which laid out the five major extreme-environment engineering challenges faced when building habitation modules, landing pads, roads, and protective domes while minimizing raw-material transport cost from Earth (tens of thousands of dollars per $1 kg$) and utilizing lunar in-situ resources (In Situ Resource Utilization, ISRU) at 100 % — namely 1/6 low gravity, ultra-high vacuum of $10^{-12} torr$, extreme thermal cycling of $-150 \sim +120 °C$, space radiation and micrometeorite impacts, and abrasive lunar dust — together with the design principles of the structural materials that respond to them (waterless sulfur concrete, sintered regolith blocks, inflatable hybrid structures).

## Key Contributions
- **Systematization of the five major extreme-environment factors of lunar surface construction and the corresponding structural design requirements**: establishing guidelines against rapid moisture evaporation under vacuum, cryogenic brittle fracture, and radiation degradation shielding.
- **Comparative evaluation of three major candidate ISRU construction material groups**: (1) melt-sintered regolith blocks, (2) waterless molten sulfur concrete (Sulfur concrete), (3) magnesia/geopolymer hydration-solidified bodies.
- **Elucidation of the mechanical/sensor wear mechanisms of lunar dust**: presenting protection measures for construction equipment and hermetic airlocks against electrostatic adhesion and abrasive angular nanoparticles.
- **Establishment of phased lunar base construction scenarios**: (Phase 1) unmanned robotic landing-pad compaction, (Phase 2) installation of inflatable habitation modules with a regolith overburden of 2 m, (Phase 3) dry assembly of permanent structures.

## Methods
- Evaluation of Apollo program lunar surface soil analysis data and ground-based simulation experiments.
- Analysis of simulated chamber test results under extreme vacuum ($10^{-9} torr$) and thermal shock.
- Numerical structural safety analysis of lunar landing loads and internal pressurization ($101 kPa$).
