# FRP composites for construction — state-of-the-art review (ASCE JCC sesquicentennial)

## Summary

A general review of FRP in construction written by nine members of the Journal of Composites
for Construction editorial board for ASCE's 150th anniversary (Bakis coordinating, with Bank,
Cosenza, Lesko, Davalos, Brown, Triantafillou, Rizkalla, and Machida; JCC 6(2), 73-87). Five
sections — structural shapes (pultrusion), highway bridge decks, internal reinforcement,
externally bonded strengthening, and codes/standards — are each organized as history →
current status → future tasks. Within this wiki it serves as the map document and first gate
for any FRP-in-construction query.

## Key Contributions

- **Structural shapes (pultrusion)**: pull-forming speeds up to 3 m/min, fiber volume fraction
  35–50 %, polyester/vinylester resins standard. Landmark projects — Aberfeldy cable-stayed
  bridge (Scotland, 114 m, 1992), Kolding Fiberline bridge (Denmark, 40 m, 1997), Basel
  Eyecatcher building (5-story residential/office, Swissbau 1999). Three challenges:
  international material specifications, consensus design codes (IBCode/AASHTO inclusion),
  cost reduction.
- **Bridge decks**: two families — sandwich type (foam or honeycomb core + E-glass faces) and
  adhesively assembled pultrusions (DuraSpan, Superdeck, EZSpan, Strongwell). Unit mass
  typically ~98 kg/m² (KSCI honeycomb 76 kg/m²); cost floor $700/m² versus conventional
  concrete decks at $322/m² — high initial cost is offset by dead-load reduction and rapid
  replacement. Deflection criteria (AASHTO L/800, plates L/300) with no FRP-specific member
  provisions are the core design uncertainty. Tasks: panel joints and anchorage, fatigue,
  combined-environment durability, local buckling, wearing surface.
- **Internal reinforcement (FRP rebar/tendons)**: linear-elastic failure (ultimate strain
  1–3 %) gives limited ductility, so serviceability (especially GFRP) usually governs design
  over strength. Over-reinforced sections (compression-controlled failure) are preferable to
  under-reinforced ones (brittle tensile rupture). Deformability index ~8 for GFRP over-
  reinforced beams; Canadian code performance factors J≥4 (rectangular)/J≥6 (T-sections);
  ACI 440 tension-controlled minimum net tensile strain ≥0.005. Shear: the customary practice
  of reducing concrete contribution by the E_FRP/E_steel modulus ratio can underestimate by a
  factor of up to 3 in deck panels; bent FRP stirrups lose strength at the bend to stress
  concentration — effective strength may fall to 35 % of straight-fiber capacity. Bond: GFRP
  pullout development lengths of 26–37 bar diameters; prestress transfer <50 tendon diameters.
  Scale of use: 32 documented FRP-rebar bridges from 1980–97 (Japan 19, Europe 6, North America 7).
- **Externally bonded strengthening**: precured strips (thickness 0.5–1.5 mm, width 50–200 mm)
  and wet lay-up sheets (<1 mm). Seven flexural failure modes classified — mode 2 (steel yield
  then concrete crushing) is best; mode 6 (adhesive-layer tension debonding) is suppressed by
  limiting FRP strain to ≈0.008. Shear-strengthening effective strain εf,e ≤ 0.004–0.005
  (fibers perpendicular to the crack), ≈0.004 for circular columns. Confinement: FRP-wrapped
  concrete shows a sharply softening then stable bilinear stress-strain curve near unconfined
  strength (dilatancy behavior, opposite to steel jackets) — ultimate is wrap rupture; square
  sections lose effectiveness through corner stress concentration. A strengthening limit
  (capacity ratio before/after stiffening) is needed for fire and accidental scenarios.
  Special techniques introduced: NSM (near-surface-mounted), prestressed strips, mechanical
  fastening.
- **Standards and design codes**: Japan (JSCE recommendations 1997, two building guidelines
  1993, spread of carbon-sheet seismic retrofit after the 1995 Hyogo-Ken Kobe earthquake, JCI
  test methods 1998); Canada (ISIS network 1995, CHBDC section 16 completed 2000, CSA building
  member code 2002); USA (ACI 440 committee founded 1991, 440R-96 state-of-report, 440.1R-01
  internal reinforcement guide, externally bonded guide 2001–02); Europe (EUROCRETE 1993–97,
  fib TG9.3 → externally bonded design bulletin 2001, UK IStructE interim guidance 1999).

## Methods

A nine-author divisional review — not experiments but synthesis of literature and realized
projects per topic. Bibliographic database searching (roughly 20 ASCE papers since 1990) was
combined with manufacturer materials (Fiberline/Creative Pultrusions/Strongwell design manuals).

## Results

General conclusion: fields with clear short-term cost-safety benefits such as external
strengthening see broad industrial adoption, while primary load-bearing applications remain
research-oriented — accumulation of long-term performance data is the precondition for
diffusion. The essential differences of FRP from metals are high strength, low stiffness, and
linear elasticity to failure; thermal expansion coefficients, moisture uptake, heat
resistance, and fire behavior also require separate treatment. Shapes and decks lag furthest
behind in standards and code development.
