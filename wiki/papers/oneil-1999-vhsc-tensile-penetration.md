# Tensile properties and penetration resistance of very-high-strength concrete (O'Neil, Neeley, Cargile, 1999)

## Summary

A paper by the U.S. Army Engineer Research and Development Center (Waterways Experiment Station, WES) that introduces VHSC, a very-high-strength concrete developed for protective structures, together with the French-lineage RPC (Reactive Powder Concrete), and reports penetration experiments on VHSC targets. Despite the 'SuperECC' in the filename, this belongs to the strength-oriented fiber-reinforced very-high-strength concrete family, not ECC. Particle packing densification, a minimum water-binder ratio, and thermal-pressure curing produce a compressive strength at the 200 MPa class, steel fibers secure toughness, and the experiments confirm an approximately 50 % decrease in penetration depth compared with ordinary concrete. It also shows that analysis by the spherical cavity expansion model agrees well with the experiments.

## Key Contributions

1. Formalization of five RPC/VHSC design principles (Table 1): homogeneity improvement through particle size and material selection, densification through particle packing and mixing technology, maximization of reactive materials and minimization of water, pressing before forming and heat treatment after curing, and incorporation of steel fibers or steel microfibers for toughness and ductility. Maximum aggregate size is 0.4 mm for RPC and 4.75 mm for VHSC.
2. Strength scale summary: up to approximately 175 MPa with room-temperature mixing and curing, beyond 200 MPa with several days of curing at 90 °C, and beyond 800 MPa with pressing plus curing at up to 400 °C (RPC). w/cm is lowered to the extreme values of 0.12 for RPC and 0.18 for VHSC, with workability secured by HRWRA.
3. Pointing out the breakdown of proportionality in tensile properties: the tensile strength of ordinary concrete is about 10 % of its compressive strength (4~10 MPa), yet even in the very-high-strength region the direct tensile strength remains at the 10 MPa level (about 10 MPa tension at the 180 MPa-class VHSC compression). The fiber-free matrix is almost linearly elastic up to fracture, and the fracture energy is below 140 J/m².
4. Fiber design: straight high-strength steel microfibers (length 13 mm, diameter 0.16 mm, 1.5~3.0 vol%) are generally optimal for RPC, and hooked steel fibers (length 30 mm, diameter 0.5 mm) for VHSC. The flexural toughness of RPC exceeds 40,000 J/m², surpassing ordinary concrete by more than 250 times.
5. Penetration test system and results: a 0.906 kg 4340 steel ogive-nose projectile (CRH 2.0, shank diameter 26.9 mm, overall length 242.4 mm, hardness Rc 43~45) was launched from a WES 83 mm smoothbore gun at impact velocities of 229~754 m/s, with a target-diameter-to-projectile-diameter ratio of about 28. The VHSC penetration depth decreased by about 50 % relative to CSPC (normal strength), while HSPC and HSFR (high-strength classes) decreased by about 30 %. Steel fibers contribute little to the penetration depth itself but significantly reduce the visible damage around the crater and rear-face spalling (scabbing).
6. Analysis: among the Forrestal-Tzou spherical cavity expansion models, the elastic-cracked-plastic (EKP) version agrees well with the experiments. The influence of the cracked region grows as the concrete strength increases, and the material tensile strength determines the cracked-region contribution among the model inputs.

## Methods

Comparison of four mixes: CSPC (normal strength, mean f'c 35 MPa, w/cm 0.57), HSPC (104 MPa, 0.22), HSFR (90 MPa, including 158 kg/m³ steel fiber, 0.30), VHSC (159 MPa, volumetric-ratio mix table, 0.18). Hardened material properties (Table 3): 28-day compressive strength of 35/104/85/157 MPa, compressive elastic modulus of 34.5/45.2/45.2/46 GPa, and 56-day tensile strength of 3.5/4.8/4.5/9.0 MPa (in the order CSPC/HSPC/HSFR/VHSC). The VHSC target was cast in a galvanized steel pipe of diameter 762 mm × length 914 mm, its circumference insulated, followed by 7 days of water curing (ponding) combined with air curing; age at testing was 30~60 days. Impact velocity was measured with a streak camera and pitch/yaw with flash X-rays, and the penetration depth P is reported in units of projectile diameter (calibers).

## Results

Penetration depth (Table 4, calibers): 3.0 at 229 m/s, 4.9 at 287 m/s, 6.0 at 397 m/s, 6.7 at 406 m/s, 10.9 at 573 m/s, 11.0 at 587 m/s, 16.5 at 747 m/s, and 17.1 at 754 m/s. In photographs of target-front damage at about 800 m/s, HSPC showed visible damage similar to CSPC (only the penetration depth reduced by 30 %), while HSFR and VHSC showed significantly smaller damage areas. The tensile strengths used as cavity expansion model inputs were set as fractions of the yield strength: CSPC 0.10, HSPC 0.0275, HSFR 0.05, VHSC 0.05. The EP (elastic-plastic) model gives smaller penetration depths than EKP, and from the difference between the two models the conclusion is drawn that the cracked-region influence is greatest in high-strength concrete.
