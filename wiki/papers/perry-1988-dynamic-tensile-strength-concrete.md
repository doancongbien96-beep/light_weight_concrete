## Summary

A classic paper that comprehensively analyzed the mechanisms by which concrete develops dynamic direct-tensile and splitting-tensile strength, together with the experimental techniques (Hopkinson bar, drop weight, explosive shock wave), over the high-speed strain-rate regime ($\dot{\epsilon} = 10^{-6} \sim 10^2 s^{-1}$). It systematized the dynamic strength increase factor (DIF) curve in which tensile strength surges to as much as 2 to 3 times the static strength as the strain rate increases, and gave physical explanations of the free-water viscous resistance (Stefan effect), crack-tip inertial confinement, and simultaneous multi-microcrack formation mechanisms.

## Key Contributions

- **Standardization of the dynamic tensile DIF curve of concrete**: formalization of the trend of dynamic tensile strength increase across the entire strain-rate range (transition rate $\dot{\epsilon}_t \approx 1.0 s^{-1}$).
- **Elucidation of the correlation between moisture content and rate sensitivity**: demonstrated that saturated concrete exhibits a much higher rate sensitivity than fully dried concrete owing to the viscous drag of free water within fine pores (Stefan effect).
- **Interpretation of the dynamic fracture-mode transition**: confirmed that at low rates cracks propagate tortuously along the aggregate-paste interfacial transition zone (ITZ), whereas at high rates the mode switches to planar fracture that penetrates the aggregates directly.

## Methods

- Meta-analysis of some 50 dynamic tensile experimental literature studies from around the world.
- Comparison of uniaxial direct tension, splitting tension (Brazilian), and flexural tension test data.
- Review of theoretical models of crack inertia and fracture-mechanics energy release rate.

## Results

- The tensile DIF responds far more sensitively to strain rate than the compressive DIF ($DIF_{tensile} > DIF_{compressive}$).
- In the region $\dot{\epsilon} > 10 s^{-1}$, the tensile strength rises sharply in exponential fashion, which has been established as an essential parameter for the design of impact- and blast-resistant structures.
