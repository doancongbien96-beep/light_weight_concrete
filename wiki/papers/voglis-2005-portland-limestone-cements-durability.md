## Summary
A two-year long-term evaluation of sulfate attack ($Na_2SO_4$ and $MgSO_4$ solutions), chloride penetration resistance, and freeze-thaw resistance of Portland limestone cement (PLC) concretes in which limestone powder replaced 10 wt %, 20 wt %, and 35 wt % of the Portland cement. The study showed that at limestone replacements up to 15 wt %, excellent sulfate resistance is maintained owing to the micro-filler effect, whereas beyond 20 wt % thaumasite formation reactions and increased porosity accelerate surface softening and spalling in magnesium sulfate environments, thereby defining the safe replacement limit for structural Portland limestone cement.

## Key Contributions
- **Clarification of the sulfate attack mechanism of limestone cements**: identified the limiting conditions under which $C-S-H$ combines with carbonate ions and degrades into thaumasite ($CaSiO_3\cdot CaCO_3\cdot CaSO_4\cdot 15H_2O$) in low-temperature ($5 °C$) sulfate environments.
- **Derivation of the critical replacement ratio for chloride penetration resistance**: within the $10 \sim 15 \%$ limestone replacement range, the charge passed remains at the $1800 C$ (Moderate) level, equivalent to plain OPC.
- **Freeze-thaw resistance assessment**: demonstrated that with proper air entrainment (air content $5.5 \%$), more than $90 \%$ of the relative dynamic modulus of elasticity is preserved after 300 cycles.

## Methods
- **Materials**: OPC and limestone-replaced cements (limestone contents 0 %, 10 %, 20 %, 35 %).
- **Long-term exposure**: immersion in $5 \% Na_2SO_4$ and $5 \% MgSO_4$ solutions (room temperature and low temperature at $5 °C$), ASTM C666 freeze-thaw testing, and ASTM C1202 RCPT testing.

## Results
- The 10 ~ 15 % limestone mixes retained excellent durability, with compressive strength loss within $12 \%$ after two years of sulfate immersion.
- The 35 % limestone mix showed severe surface expansion cracking and a $45 \%$ strength loss after one year of $MgSO_4$ immersion $\rightarrow$ combined use of pozzolans such as silica fume is essential in high-sulfate environments.
