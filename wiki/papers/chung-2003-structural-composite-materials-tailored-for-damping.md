# Structural composite materials tailored for damping (review)

## Summary

A review comprehensively covering tailoring strategies that raise the vibration damping capability of the structural material itself through composite engineering while minimizing the loss of stiffness (storage modulus). It extends the sources of damping beyond viscoelasticity to the slippage of defects such as dislocations, grain boundaries, and interfaces, and presents increasing interfacial area via small fillers and surface treatment as the key means. In cement systems, silica fume raises damping and stiffness simultaneously (especially effective in mortar), rebar contributes dominantly to both damping and stiffness and is improved further by sandblasting surface treatment. In continuous carbon fiber polymer composites, a 0.1 μm carbon filament insertion layer outperforms a viscoelastic interlayer in loss modulus above 50 °C.

## Key Contributions

1. **Unified frame for damping tailoring**: takes the loss modulus — the product of raising the loss factor (tan δ) and the storage modulus (E') — as the vibration-reduction performance index, and organizes a four-axis strategy: (i) interface (fiber coating/surface treatment, interlayer additives), (ii) matrix (polymer blends, admixtures for cement systems), (iii) reinforcing fibers (polymer fibers/inserted graphite fiber layers), (iv) additional fibers (hybrids of polymer/coated/shape-memory alloy fibers). Additives with viscoelasticity or small particle size are advantageous — small particles maximize interfacial area, enlarging interfacial slippage energy dissipation during vibration.
2. **Quantitative cement-system benchmark (three-point bending, room temperature)**: plain cement paste tan δ 0.035 (0.2 Hz) and E' 1.9 GPa → adding as-received silica fume (~0.1 μm, 15 mass %) gives tan δ 0.082 and E' 12.7 GPa; the silane-treated grade gives 0.087 and 16.8 GPa — simultaneous large gains in damping and stiffness. Adding treated silica fume to mortar (plain E' 20~26 GPa) raises E' to 32~33 GPa — the silica fume effect is even larger in the presence of sand.
3. **Fibers are ineffective; rebar is dominant**: adding 0.5 vol % carbon fiber (15 μm, ~5 mm), carbon filament (0.1 μm, longer than 100 μm), or steel fiber (8 μm, 6 mm) to paste is ineffective-to-marginal for paste damping (the damping from the inherent heterogeneity of cement paste is already large), and steel fiber (0.5~1.0 vol %) actually lowers tan δ to 0.046~0.051 — interfacial area beats fiber ductility. By contrast, steel rebar raises mortar tan δ from below 0.001 to 0.027 (0.2 Hz) and E' from 20 to 44 GPa; sandblasting improves it further to tan δ 0.037 and E' 46 GPa (0.012 and 49 GPa on a 1 Hz basis) — demonstrating the role of surface deformation patterns (ribs) and roughness.
4. **Dual action of silane surface treatment**: hydrophilizes silica fume, improving wetting and dispersion in aqueous cement mixes. Applied to fume it raises tan δ and E' simultaneously; applied to carbon fiber it raises E' only. Incorporating silane directly as an admixture gives lower damping and compressive ductility than coating plus increased drying shrinkage — attributed to formation of covalently bonded networks between particles. Latex (styrene-butadiene dispersion) incorporation delivers top-level tan δ 0.142 (0.2 Hz) and 0.112 (1 Hz), but it costs more than silica fume, limiting practicality.
5. **Polymer-composite interlayer design (nylon-6 matrix + continuous carbon fiber 7 μm)**: inserting a viscoelastic sheet (55 μm) raises longitudinal tan δ from 0.008 to 0.43 (0.2 Hz) but collapses E' from 127 to 7 GPa. An ozone-treated (160 °C surface oxidation) carbon filament insertion layer holds tan δ 0.09 with E' 115 GPa, preserving stiffness. As temperature rises, the viscoelastic layer's tan δ and loss modulus plunge from 20 to 50 °C while the filament layer declines only gently, so at 50 °C and 100 °C the treated-filament composite shows the highest loss modulus — the basis for high-temperature damping design.
6. **Matrix selection criteria for damping (1 Hz)**: PTFE (tan δ 0.224, E' 1.34 GPa) and PMMA (0.100, 3.49 GPa) are structural candidates (epoxy 0.039, PA-66 0.078; neoprene rubber reaches tan δ 1.12 but its E' of 7.83 MPa makes it nonstructural). Interfaces between constituents of polymer blends and interpenetrating networks are also promising damping mechanisms.

## Methods

Review paper. The collected data rest on three-point bending dynamic mechanical testing (0.2 Hz and 1.0 Hz); continuous-fiber polymer composites were measured in longitudinal (fibers bent) versus transverse (not bent) configurations, the longitudinal one being closer to real structural composites. Part of the cement-table data (the carbon filament and steel fiber rows) is stated to come from the Chung group's unpublished results ([74], [75]). Table 1 organizes the cement paste vs mortar distinction (absence/presence of sand) and the addition levels, e.g. silica fume 15 mass %, carbon fiber 0.5 vol %, and steel fiber 0.5~1.0 vol %.

## Results

Representative cement-system values (tan δ / E', 0.2 Hz, room-temperature three-point bending):

| Material | tan δ | E' (GPa) |
|---|---|---|
| Cement paste (plain) | 0.035 | 1.9 |
| Paste + silica fume (15 mass %, as-received) | 0.082 | 12.7 |
| Paste + treated silica fume | 0.087 | 16.8 |
| Paste + treated carbon fiber + treated silica fume | 0.083 | 21 |
| Paste + treated carbon filament + treated silica fume | 0.106 | 11.3 |
| Paste + steel fiber 0.5 vol % + silica fume | 0.051 | 12.9 |
| Paste + latex | 0.142 | – |
| Mortar (plain) | below 0.001 | 20 |
| Mortar + treated silica fume | 0.011 | 32 |
| Mortar + as-received rebar | 0.027 | 44 |
| Mortar + sandblasted rebar | 0.037 | 46 |
| Mortar + rebar + treated silica fume | 0.027 | 47 |

- Sand lowers damping considerably but is essential for drying-shrinkage control, so practical mixes combine it with silica fume and rebar.
- Polymer composites (longitudinal, 0.2 Hz): no interlayer tan δ 0.008 and E' 127 GPa → viscoelastic sheet 0.43 and 7 GPa, as-received filament 0.007 and 65 GPa, treated filament 0.09 and 115 GPa. Loss modulus peaks at room temperature for the viscoelastic sheet: 16 GPa (0.2 Hz) and 23.5 GPa (1 Hz).
- Temperature dependence (0.2 Hz, longitudinal): the viscoelastic interlayer's tan δ drops sharply from 20 to 50 °C, whereas filament systems decline only slightly. At 50 °C and 100 °C the highest loss modulus belongs to the treated-filament interlayer composite.
