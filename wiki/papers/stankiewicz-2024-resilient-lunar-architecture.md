## Summary
A recent review and concept design paper that comprehensively analyzes the latest in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) construction materials, 3D printing additive manufacturing methods, and modular structural systems for lunar base construction. To cope with the extreme lunar environment (one-sixth gravity, $-180 \sim +120 °C$ thermal cycles, high vacuum of $10^{-10} Pa$, space radiation, and micrometeorite impacts), it comparatively analyzed manufacturing technologies that sinter lunar regolith, convert it into sulfur/geopolymer binders, or melt it with microwaves. It demonstrated that a hybrid structure combining a 3D-printed regolith dome shell with an internal inflatable pressurized habitat provides optimal structural safety and radiation shielding performance.

## Key Contributions
- **Construction of a comparison matrix of the four major ISRU methods for lunar construction materials**: comparison of energy demand and strength across (1) solar/laser-sintered regolith, (2) microwave-melted consolidated solids, (3) waterless sulfur concrete, and (4) alkali-activated regolith geopolymer.
- **Sizing of radiation and micrometeorite protection shield thickness**: specification of the minimum regolith shield layer thickness ($2.0 \sim 3.0 m$) required to satisfy the annual permissible radiation dose ($< 50 mSv/yr$).
- **Presentation of a roadmap for autonomous robotic 3D printing construction**: systematization of extrusion/sintering print control strategies under extreme vacuum and temperature gradients.

## Methods
- Comprehensive meta-analysis of recent lunar exploration (Artemis program) literature and ISRU materials experimental data.
- Review of heat transfer analyses and Monte Carlo radiation transport (GCR, SPE) simulation data.

## Results
- Regolith geopolymers and sintered bodies: compressive strengths of $25 \sim 45 MPa$ attainable, with permanent durability confirmed without chemical sublimation in the lunar vacuum environment.
- A dual-protection system erecting a 3D-printed regolith vault/dome outside an inflatable membrane structure emerged as the standard architecture for future permanent lunar bases.
