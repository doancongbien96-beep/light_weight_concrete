# Effect of mix constituent variations on UHPC cost and performance

## Summary

A study by the El-Tawil group at the University of Michigan that evaluated compressive and direct-tension behavior using silica fume (SF) content, silica powder (SP) content, cement type (white Type I / Portland Type V / GGBS-Type I blend), and steel fiber volume fraction (0.5/1.0/1.5 %) as experimental variables, targeting minimum material cost for non-proprietary UHPC. It is effectively the first systematic study of UHPC with fiber contents below 1 %; the effect of constituent changes on performance was found to be generally small (explained by proximity to the MA grading model), and the practical levers of cost reduction were identified as eliminating SP and using blended GGBS cement.

## Key Contributions

1. Demonstration of strain-hardening in low-fiber-content (<1 %) UHPC: hardening occurs even at 0.5 % steel fiber (strain capacity 0.07 %), 0.13 % at 1.0 %, and 0.23 % at 1.5 %. With increasing fiber content, compressive strength (157→183 MPa), post-cracking strength (σpc 5.8→8.9 MPa), and crack count (2.2→9.5 cracks) increase linearly, while fiber tensile stress (σfpc 1287→657 MPa) decreases (fiber clustering effect).
2. Structural application criterion proposed: from rebar yield strain 0.2 % and ASCE 7 load factors (dead 1.4, live 1.7), rebar strain is estimated at ~0.09 % under dead load and ~0.13 % under service load. Allowing for the fiber-alignment bias of coupon tests, 0.5 % falls short, 1.0 % prevents local cracking in the dead-load phase, and raising the content to 1.5 % provides protection through the service-load phase.
3. Constituent sensitivity conclusions: raising SF 25→35 % changes compression by <1.5 % and tension by ~11 % (a mild deterioration); varying SP over 0~25 % stays within ±5 % for both tension and compression, so expensive SP ($186/m3) can be eliminated. Among cement types, white cement performs best (compression 191 MPa, σpc 9.3 MPa) but is also the most expensive.
4. Cost analysis frame: introduction of a cost index relative to the base mix (Wille et al. 2011, 1C:0.25SF:0.25SP, w/b=0.22). Each 1 vol% of fiber adds about $680/m3. The cheapest mix GG-25-00 (GGBS/Type I, zero SP) reaches an index of 0.52, a 48 % saving while retaining ultra-high performance: this study's recommended mix.
5. Explanation via the MA (Modified Andreasen) grading model (q=0.37): the particle size distributions of all mixes remain within ±5 % of the ideal curve, supporting the insensitivity of performance to constituent changes.

## Methods

- Cement fixed at 775 kg/m3, w/c=0.22. Direct tension on dumbbell specimens per AASHTO T 132-87 (cross-section 25 mm2, gauge length 80 mm), strain rate ~0.0001 /s, alignment after preload to 20 % of matrix cracking strength (Pyo's method), ≥6 specimens per mix. Cracks visualized by applying isopropyl alcohol plus blue dye.
- Compression on 50.8 mm cubes (ASTM C109), ≥6 per mix. Following Naaman's HPFRCC classification, σcc, σpc, εpc, energy absorption γ (area up to 95 % σpc), Ecc, and σfpc=σpc/(0.9Vf) were evaluated. Steel fibers: brass-coated straight type (19 mm, d 0.2 mm, ≥1965 MPa).

## Results

- Mean compressive strength: white 191 > GGBS/I 180 > Type V 179 MPa; all three meet the UHPC threshold (150 MPa). Mean σpc 9.3/8.9/8.4 MPa and εpc 0.21~0.24 %: differences among cement types negligible.
- SP 0→25 %: compression 173→187 MPa (slightly below trend at SF 20 %); σpc, crack count, and fiber stress essentially unchanged. No effect commensurate with SP's high cost.
- SF 25→35 %: compression 184→186 MPa, σpc 9.0→8.0 MPa, slight decreases in energy absorption and fiber stress. Excess SF erodes the matrix's ability to transfer load to fibers on the tension side.
- Compressive strength scales linearly with the cost index (cost order: white > blended > Type V).
