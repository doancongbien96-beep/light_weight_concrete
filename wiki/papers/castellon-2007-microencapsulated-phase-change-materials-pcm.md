# Microencapsulated PCM Concrete for Building Applications — Full-Scale Cubicle Thermal Performance Verification

## Summary

An EU MOPCON project study by the Universitat de Lleida (Cabeza group). Commercial microencapsulated PCM with a melting point of 26 °C and phase-change enthalpy of 110 kJ/kg was mixed into concrete used to build the south wall, west wall, and roof of a full-scale (house-size) cubicle erected at Puigverd de Lleida, Spain, then monitored simultaneously against a same-condition PCM-free reference cubicle through summer and autumn 2005. Measured data confirm that the PCM wall peaks 3 °C lower with its peak delayed by about 2 h, and that heat-flux behavior changes completely within the phase-change window. Mechanical strength is lower than ordinary concrete but satisfies non-structural wall requirements (structural walls sit too close to the limit and are not recommended).

## Key Contributions

- Full-scale verification of PCM-mixed concrete: with the outdoor peak at 32 °C, the west-wall peak temperature dropped from 39 °C (PCM-free) to 36 °C — a 3 °C reduction — with the same margin at minimum temperatures. Wall peak arrival was delayed by about 2 h (increased thermal inertia), appearing during both the afternoon solidification and morning melting periods.
- Separating performance by window-opening condition: when windows stayed open all day, the thermal-inertia effect tied to PCM solidification was not clearly evident — measured evidence that user behavior (ventilation patterns) is the variable governing PCM performance and energy-savings effect.
- Necessity of night cooling confirmed: for all the PCM in the walls to complete a full daily melting-solidification cycle, night ventilation cooling is essential.
- Heat-flux sensing on the south-wall inner surface: outside the melting/solidification windows the two cubicles show the same heat-flux trends, whereas during phase change the behavior changes completely (Figures 5-7).
- Blind experiments separating measurement artifacts: without blinds, direct sunlight striking the sensor produced indoor spike peaks that disappear once blinds are installed (Figure 11 vs 12).
- Mechanical performance limits stated explicitly: both compressive and flexural strength degrade relative to ordinary concrete; non-structural wall criteria are met, but structural walls sit too close to the current lower limit to be recommended — improving PCM-concrete strength is follow-up work.

## Methods

- Materials: new mixes incorporating commercial microencapsulated PCM (melting point 26 °C, ΔH = 110 kJ/kg) into concrete for the south wall, west wall, and roof. An adjacent identical-geometry, identically oriented PCM-free cubicle operated simultaneously as the control.
- Instrumentation: temperature sensors on each wall; indoor air temperature sensors at 1.2 m and 2.0 m heights at the cubicle center; one heat-flux sensor on the south-wall inner surface; two pyranometers at the top of the cubicles; a nearby weather station — all collected via data logger to PC.
- Test cases: (1) closed windows, (2) open around the clock (24 h), (3) open at night only (pre-cooling), each repeated with and without blinds (July-August 2005).

## Results

- West wall (closed windows, late August 2005): PCM wall 36 °C vs PCM-free 39 °C (outdoor 32 °C), a difference of 3 °C — identical at minimum temperatures.
- Heat flux (south wall): within ±3.5 W/m², the two cubicles' trends separate depending on whether phase change is under way.
- Blinds present + windows open all day: direct-sun spikes vanish and the indoor temperature curves of both cubicles smooth out.
- Follow-up: a Trombe wall added to the south facade (targeting simultaneous reductions in cooling and heating demand in a Mediterranean climate); experiments with internal heat sources (people, computers) and autonomous heat-pump control planned.
