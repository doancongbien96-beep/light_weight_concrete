# Recent Advances in Magnesium-Based Binders: CO2 Sequestration and Utilization, Mechanical Properties, and Environmental Impact

## Summary

Despite the filename prefix, the document bibliography identifies this as Dan Meng's review (19 pp.) in Cement and Concrete Composites. It is a systematic state-of-the-art review centered on reactive MgO cement (RMC) and magnesium oxychloride cement (MOC), organizing the CO2 sequestration mechanisms of magnesium-based binders, carbonation control factors, quantitative analysis of sequestered amounts, and environmental impact versus PC (LCA). RMC's theoretical carbon sequestration capacity is 110 %, roughly twice that of OPC (46~58 %), and the fact that carbonation couples with strength development is presented as the key differentiator from conventional PC-system carbonation (C-S-H decalcification with porosity increase). Starting from the premise that the cement industry accounts for about 7 % of global CO2 emissions, it proposes a low-carbon alternative binder roadmap.

## Key Contributions

- Comparison table of seven magnesium-based binder families (RMC·MOC·RMS·MOS·BMS·MPC·Novacem) by composition, strength range, and pros/cons: RMC 20~70 MPa, MOC 60~150 MPa (28 days).
- RMC carbonation mechanism: brucite is a weak phase, so carbonation builds strength. Formation and transition pathways of the four HMC phases (artinite·nesquehonite·hydromagnesite·dypingite) and their morphology-strength correlation (acicular nesquehonite networks outperform rosette-like hydromagnesite·dypingite on strength).
- Quantitative roadmap of carbonation control factors (Results below). In particular, over-carbonation causes volume expansion → internal stress → strength loss, making 7~14 days the optimal window to reach maturity strength.
- Additive strategies systematized: hydration accelerators (magnesium acetate up to +273 %), carbonation accelerators (NaHCO3 ×4.2, sodium sesquicarbonate Na3H(CO3)2·2H2O ×6.3, ureolytic bacteria ×3), hydromagnesite nucleation seeding (+33~142 %), dispersant NaHMP (+61 %), and cellulose·sisal fibers acting as CO2 diffusion pathways.
- Comparison of sequestration quantification methodologies: phenolphthalein (underestimates via its pH 9.0 transition point), MIP, XCT (brucite-HMC gray-level separation difficult), QXRD (RIR·Rietveld), TGA-DTA deconvolution, TG-IR. Literature CO2 sequestration ratios of 4.6~43 % → up to 430 kg CO2 offset per tonne of RMC.
- LCA comparison: RMC production (dry route 1360~1600 kg CO2-eq/t) is about 66 % higher than PC (811~977), yet crediting the measured 430 kg sequestration gives a net 930~1170 kg, and crediting the theoretical capacity of 110 % reverses the ranking to 260~500 kg CO2-eq. The wet route (starting from seawater or reject brine) carries long-term carbon-negative potential because its feedstock contains no fossil CO2. Among Miller & Myers' 13 alternative binders, forsterite-derived MgO ranks lowest at −0.33 kg CO2/kg cement.
- As future directions it proposes strain-hardening RMC·MOC fiber-reinforced composites suited to low-alkalinity environments (steel reinforcement could be omitted) — the review closes by citing Ruan-Qiu-Yang-Unluer 2018 CCC, Wu-Zhang-Ellis-Li 2018 CBM (carbonation-cured MgO-ECC), and Qiu-Ruan-Unluer-Yang 2019 CCR (MgO-based SHCC self-healing).

## Results

- Quantitative conclusions on control factors: optimum w/c 0.5~0.8 (the diffusivity of CO2 in water, 0.0016 mm²/s, is one ten-thousandth of its 16 mm²/s in air, so excess moisture hinders diffusion); RH ~78 % (moderate-to-high w/c 0.5~0.7) or ~98 % (low w/c); maximum strength gain rate at a CO2 concentration of 5~10 % (+109 % at 10 %, yield falls off sharply above that); pressure effective at 0.4~0.55 MPa and marginal up to 1 MPa; supercritical CO2 (9.5 MPa·35 °C·4 h) reaching 46 MPa early and tripling the sequestration ratio (2.8→8.8 %); precuring at 50~60 °C for 1~2 days adding +25~35 % to 28-day strength; curing maturing at 7~14 days (longer periods beyond 14 days are unprofitable or counterproductive).
- PC-RMC blends: 60~70 % RMC replacement is optimal (porosity 3~4 %, compressive strength 67~84 MPa); distinct nesquehonite formation threshold at 60 %.
- MOC carbonation: dissolution of phase 5·3 → formation of chlorartinite Mg2(CO3)Cl(OH)·3H2O, then transition to the stable hydromagnesite phase. With 10 % CO2, the equivalent of 15 years of atmospheric exposure is secured in 6 weeks; with 100 % CO2, in 2 weeks. The rate surges above RH 59 % (at 35 % it is negligible). Passive carbonation rate of 0.07 kg CO2/m²/year (cumulative 1 kg CO2/m² over 15 years). CO2 curing improves water resistance through M-Cl-C-H gel formation but is ineffective against expansion (volume stability).
- LCA intensities: PC 811~977, RMC dry 1360~1600, RMC wet (pyrohydrolysis) 860~19150 kg CO2-eq/t. On a concrete basis, PC 228 vs MOC dry 308·wet 285 kg CO2-eq/t (35 %·25 % higher respectively), yet on the damage single score dry-route MOC falls below PC. A Swedish CO2 tax case (139 EUR/t) supports pairing policy instruments.
